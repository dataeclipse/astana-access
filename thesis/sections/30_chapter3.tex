\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the results in the order of the hypotheses. Unless stated otherwise, population is the polling-station model of Section~\ref{sec:population}, children are of school age for schools and of preschool age for preschools, and walking distances are measured along the network.

\textbf{Statutory compliance.} Table~\ref{tab:compliance} and Figure~\ref{fig:compliance} give the share of children living within the radius of the planning standard. Read as a straight-line radius, the standard is met for 64 per cent of school-age children and 77 per cent of preschool-age children. Read as a walking distance, the shares fall to 22 and 34 per cent. The gap is not a technicality: superblocks, fenced courtyards, rivers and highways lengthen the walk to a median of 1.41 times the straight line, and the norm that a planner checks on a map is met for a minority of children on foot. Across the three building-based population surfaces the city shares vary by less than 2.5 points. District shares do not depend on the age structure, which is uniform within a district; the city shares do.

\textbf{Do families use the nearest school?} The education department assigns every address of the city to a school. Its lists for all 121 schools with a service territory \cite{catchments2026} were parsed and geocoded (Section~\ref{sec:catchments}); 13,764 dwellings, home to 56 per cent of school-age children, are matched to their assigned school. For 62 per cent of these children the assigned school is the nearest by walking distance and for 83 per cent one of the two nearest. The median walk to the assigned school is 786\,m; 20 per cent of children live within the statutory 500\,m of it, 67 per cent within 1\,km and 87 per cent within 1.5\,km. In Esil and Nura only 58 and 52 per cent live within 1\,km of their assigned school, against 82 per cent in Baikonur. Catchments are administrative, not geometric: four children in ten are sent past a nearer school. This is why the accessibility index below lets a child reach every school within walking distance rather than assigning each child to the nearest one.

The same lists and the official enrolment test the load step of the index. The number of children assigned to each school correlates with the load the index allocates to it at 0.53 to 0.57 (Spearman, 115 schools) for allocation radii between 1.5 and 4\,km, and at 0.53 when every child is counted at every school within reach, as in the original method; this test is weakened by the 44 per cent of children whose dwellings could not be matched to a territory. The enrolment of 119 schools in 2025--2026 \cite{schoolregister2026} is the more direct test: 0.60 for the allocated load and 0.41 for the original method. Allocating each child once is the better description of how schools are loaded; the choice of allocation radius matters little.

\textbf{How full are the schools?} The official list counts 1.7 pupils per design place. Forty-five of the 120 schools enrol more than twice their capacity, more than two full shifts can hold, and 49 per cent of pupils attend them; 18,486 pupils are beyond that limit. Saraishyk is the most crowded district, with 2.0 pupils per place, 75 per cent of its pupils in such schools and 6,445 pupils beyond two shifts, followed by Esil and Nura with 58 and 54 per cent of pupils in such schools; Almaty is the least crowded, with 22 per cent. Enrolment grows fastest in Nura, by 17 per cent from 2025--2026 to 2026--2027, and by 11 per cent in Saraishyk and Esil. A shortage of places and a shortage of walking access are different deficits: the first concerns how many places there are, the second where they stand.

\textbf{How full are the preschools?} The cards of the register count 66,574 attending children for 73,419 places, a median of 0.94 children per place; 148 organisations are exactly full and 58 above capacity, and Saraishyk is the fullest district with 0.96 children per place \cite{indigocards2026}. Attendance follows capacity (Spearman 0.95), not the children who live around a preschool: the load the index allocates to each organisation does not correlate with its attendance (0.00, 723 organisations). Preschools are chosen, through the city-wide queue and vouchers, rather than assigned by territory, so for preschools the index measures potential access, not use.

For schools, H2 is supported at the level of district means. With official design capacity as supply, the two districts of recent growth are the worst served: Nura, where 46 per cent of residential floor area was built after 2022, has 76 per cent of the city mean and the lowest walking compliance, 14 per cent, and Esil 81 per cent, with the largest deficit cluster of the city: 4.6 per cent of all school-age children live in significant low-low clusters in Esil, against 1.8 in Nura (Figure~\ref{fig:access-school}). With floor area as supply, as in earlier versions of the model, Esil stood at 90 per cent. Almaty and Saraishyk carry their combined age share; under the official split Almaty would fall to 87 per cent of the mean and Saraishyk rise to 133. Enrolment rejects that split: under it the index allocates 38 per cent more pupils than are enrolled to the schools of Almaty and 32 per cent fewer to those of Saraishyk, under the combined share 11 per cent more and 6 per cent fewer, and the rank correlation with enrolment is 0.60 against 0.55. Differences between districts remain modest: 8 per cent of inequality lies between them.

The supply follows the official list of service territories. It contains six Binom schools, private schools financed by state order, which the education department assigns territories like state schools, and the Daryn lyceum, which also has one; an earlier version of the model treated all seven as selective and excluded them. Six of the seven stand in Esil and Nura, and their inclusion raises Esil from 54 to 81 per cent of the mean. OSM maps two objects as school 45; the official list of schools shows that it moved in 2025 into a Comfortable School building in Nura, so its old building in Esil is left out of the present supply and kept in that of 2023. Every number in the official list is matched in the supply layer or among the programme schools, and every state school number listed in 2GIS \cite{twogis2026} is either in the layer, a selective or evening school or a programme school. Removing the five presumed new schools lowers Nura to 73 per cent of the mean.

For preschools, H2 is supported in direction. With design capacity as supply, Nura, the fastest-growing district, has the lowest accessibility, 77 per cent of the mean, followed closely by Saraishyk, the district of new housing separated from Almaty in 2024, at 79, and Esil at 88; with mean attendance per organisation, as in earlier versions of the model, Nura and Saraishyk stood at 83 and Esil at 94 per cent. Under the official age split Saraishyk would rise to 118 per cent. Differences between districts remain small: 9 per cent of inequality lies between them (Figure~\ref{fig:access-preschool}), and the deficit is mostly a matter of blocks. An earlier version of the model, which took the age structure from WorldPop, placed the deficit in Baikonur at 64 per cent of the mean; the official age counts show that Baikonur has the smallest share of preschool children in the city, 7.3 per cent against 12.6, and the apparent deficit disappears. The age structure of the population is as consequential for the conclusions as its spatial distribution.

In both cases most of the inequality lies within districts, 92 per cent for schools and 91 per cent for preschools (Equation~\eqref{eq:theil}), and accessibility is strongly clustered. Planning that compares district averages, the form in which provision is usually reported, misses most of the inequality the cell-level surface reveals. Figure~\ref{fig:lorenz} shows the Lorenz curves.

\label{sec:results-h3}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} summarises the 72 runs per facility type. For schools the worst-served district is Nura in every one of the 54 building-based runs, and district rankings correlate with the baseline at 0.94 in the median. For preschools the worst-served district is Saraishyk in 32 of the 54 building-based runs, Nura in 12, Saryarka in 6 and Baikonur in 4; with design capacity it is Saraishyk in 12 of 18 runs and Nura in 6, two districts that differ by two points of the mean in the baseline, and the median correlation of rankings is 0.71. For both facility types H3 holds at the cell level across the building-based surfaces, and for schools also at the district level: Nura is the worst-served district in every building-based run. For preschools the two worst districts, Nura and Saraishyk, trade places across runs, which is consistent with a deficit that lies mostly within districts. It does not hold at the cell level when population comes from WorldPop: the cells it marks as worst served overlap those of the building-based surfaces by only 14 per cent. Supply measure, decay function and catchment size move individual cells; the choice of population surface moves the deficit itself. This is the practical consequence of H1: an assessment that takes its population from the standard gridded product would direct new facilities to different places.

\textbf{Uncertainty of the population surface.} The 72 specifications vary modelling choices; the population surface also carries a random error, which was propagated by a Monte Carlo simulation of 200 draws. Each draw takes the exponent $\alpha$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:allocation} uniformly between 0 and 0.3, multiplies the size of each of the 340 polling stations with dwellings by an independent log-normal factor, and keeps each of the 33,818 dwellings decided by the classifier with its predicted probability; district totals stay official. The spread of station sizes, $\sigma = 0.85$ on the log scale, is set so that it alone would explain the 10 per cent by which the model misses district totals, which makes it an upper bound. Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty} gives the intervals. Nura is the worst-served district for schools in 150 of 200 draws and Esil in 49, so one of the two growth districts is the worst served in all but one; for preschools Nura and Saraishyk again share the last place, in 102 and 93 draws. The reserved sites reach more children than the plan in every draw, and with the 17 sites fixed at the baseline choice at least twice as many in 90 per cent of draws.

\section{Backtest Against the Comfortable School Programme: Test of H4}
\label{sec:results-h4}

\subsection{The programme as built}
\label{sec:programme-list}

Of the 24 sites announced in 2023 \cite{skc2023}, 23 were reported built \cite{skc2024}, and the official list of schools \cite{schoolregister2026} marks 23 Comfortable School buildings in Astana with 42,400 places: 14 opened in 2024, eight in 2025 and one in 2026. They are schools 99 to 119 and two older numbers, 33, a new school, and 45, which moved into its new building in 2025; these two match in year and district the two announced sites that eleven public sources could not locate. All 23 are in the supply layer: 18 through the OSM school of the same number, three through new unnamed school grounds confirmed by the address or the listing of the same school \cite{twogis2026}, and two, numbers 105 and 116, which OSM does not yet map, at their listed position. School 105 had first been placed at the street crossing named in the announcement, 1.07\,km from where it stands. The same list corrects the city of 2023: schools 94 to 97 opened in 2023 or later outside the programme, and school 45 still stood in its old building in Esil.

\subsection{Were sites chosen by deficit or by density?}

Against the city of 1 January 2023, the deficit score singles out the programme cells with a ROC-AUC of 0.59 at both 1 and 1.5\,km, with intervals from 0.45 to 0.72, while density scores 0.42, no better than chance. The difference is positive in 98 and 96 per cent of bootstrap samples, with 95 per cent intervals from $+0.00$ to $+0.31$ and from $-0.03$ to $+0.37$. The one predictor that clearly singles out the chosen sites is the availability of a plot: 16 of the 23 sites had a feasible plot in 2023, against 29 per cent of all cells (ROC-AUC 0.71, interval 0.61 to 0.80), and among cells with a plot the deficit scores 0.71 at 1\,km. The first part of H4 holds in direction: the programme went where land was available and, among such places, where children lacked a school; but with 23 sites the preference for deficit over density is borderline, its interval touching zero at 1\,km and including it at 1.5\,km.

\textbf{A multivariate model of the choice.} Taken together, the nine features of Section~\ref{sec:method-siting} single out the programme cells with a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.71 (0.60 to 0.81) for the logistic model and 0.63 for LightGBM, no better than the plot alone. The distance to a feasible plot is the only feature whose standardised coefficient excludes zero, $-1.02$ ($-1.52$ to $-0.60$), and it leads the SHAP ranking ahead of the deficit within 1\,km, whose coefficient is positive but includes zero. Applied to 2026, the model does not anticipate the plan (ROC-AUC 0.55, 0.41 to 0.71): the city chose differently in its next round. What singles out the 17 new schools of the plan is construction: 16 of them lie within 500\,m of land under construction, against 41 per cent of all cells (ROC-AUC 0.76, 0.68 to 0.84). The plan builds schools with new housing estates rather than where children already lack one.

\subsection{Does the construction plan for 2026--2028 follow the deficit?}
\label{sec:plan}

In reply to an information request, the city's construction department listed 25 school projects to be completed from 2026 to 2028 \cite{constructionplan2026}: 18 new schools, three extensions and four new buildings for existing schools, with 70,316 places in two shifts, of which 8,150 are due in 2026, 29,566 in 2027 and 32,600 in 2028. The list gives a street crossing, an address, a street or the number of the school being replaced, and was located in the same way as the programme: 24 of the 25 projects are placed, 14 with high, four with medium and six with low confidence; one new school is named after a street that neither OSM nor any address list contains. Each new school is then given the coverage test of Section~\ref{sec:results-h4} with the demand of 2026, its own capacity and the existing schools fixed; the plan is compared with the optimum for the same number of sites, and the selection test is repeated for the planned cells.

Table~\ref{tab:plan} and Figure~\ref{fig:plan} give the result. By 2028 the plan brings a school within 1\,km of 11,904 of the 77,870 children who lack one, 15 per cent, and within 1.5\,km of 19 per cent; capacity never binds. The deficit does not single out the planned cells: its ROC-AUC is 0.56 at 1\,km, with an interval from 0.40 to 0.71, no different from density at 0.56. The districts tell the same story. Esil has the most children without a school within 1\,km, 21,521, and 14,334 of the 32,197 children in significant low-access clusters of Figure~\ref{fig:access-school}, but its three planned schools reach 793 of them; Saraishyk has 9,616 and receives the most places, 22,800. Within 1\,km of all new schools live 7 per cent of the children in low-access clusters. Two sites lie where few children live today and anticipate housing under construction, and four lie inside areas already within 1\,km of a school, three of them next to schools with more than two pupils per place, where they would relieve crowding rather than extend walking access.

The official enrolment adds a reason. The plan follows crowding: Saraishyk, with 6,445 of the city's 18,486 pupils beyond what two shifts can hold, receives the most places, and over the six districts planned places correlate with these pupils at 0.71 (Spearman) and with the children who lack a school within 1\,km at 0.60, while Esil, with the largest walking deficit, receives half as many places as Saraishyk. Within districts the sites are not placed by crowding either: the pupils beyond two shifts at schools within 1.5\,km single out the planned cells with a ROC-AUC of 0.49, the ratio of pupils to places with 0.58.

The plan reaches more children than the optimum on plots that the land layer finds free, 108 per cent of it at 1\,km, because the city plans schools on land the layer counts as built, such as a garage cooperative in Almaty that is to be cleared: land can be made, not only found. Against the optimum anywhere the plan reaches 31 per cent at 1\,km and 25 per cent at 1.5\,km. Adding the places of all 24 projects to the floating catchment index raises mean access by 33 per cent and lowers the share of children without any school in reach from 6.6 to 5.3 per cent and the Gini coefficient from 0.378 to 0.368: Nura gains, from 0.76 to 1.00 of the city mean, Esil from 0.81 to 0.87, Saraishyk from 0.94 to 1.17, and Almaty falls from 1.05 to 0.92. For future decisions, then, the first part of H4 does not hold for walking access: the plan answers crowding by district more than the walking deficit, and about 85 per cent of the children now without a school within 1\,km would still be without one in 2028.

\textbf{Demand at completion.} The plan is judged against the children of 2026, but its schools open in 2027 and 2028. Projected to 2028 with the cohort change ratios of the official single-age tables for 2024--2026 \cite{statages2026}, the Hamilton--Perry method, with Almaty and Saraishyk pooled, the school-age population of the city grows from 311,000 to about 392,000, and to about 333,000 if children only grow older without migration; in the first case Nura grows by 85 per cent. Holding the pattern within each district, the children without a school within 1\,km rise from 77,870 to between 84,100 and 103,300; the plan reaches 15 to 17 per cent of them, 71,200 to 85,700 remain without one, and the 17 best reserved sites would reach 2.2 to 2.4 times as many as the plan. This projection spreads the growth of each district over its present dwellings; the forecast of new housing below places it in the estates being built.

\textbf{Where the children of 2028 will live.} The housing model ranks cells well by whether housing appears and less well by how much: trained on 2020--2023 and tested on 2023--2026, its ROC-AUC is 0.94 against 0.92 for the rule that construction continues where it was, but its top tenth of cells captures 76 per cent of the new floor area against 81 per cent for that rule; cross-validated within 2023--2026 it reaches 0.93 and 83 per cent. Its training period is weakened by OSM itself, since much of what appears between 2017 and 2020 is old housing mapped late, which hides the persistence of construction. The forecast puts 37 per cent of the new housing of 2026--2029 in Nura, 31 in Esil and 20 in Saraishyk. Placing in this housing the 65,000 children that migration adds by 2028, of whom 30,000 would have no school within 1\,km, raises the children without one to 112,800, against 103,300 when growth follows the present pattern; Nura alone has 41,500. The plan reaches 16 per cent of them and the 17 best reserved sites 1.9 times as many. The ranking of reserved sites is robust to this growth: 15 of the 17 sites chosen on the demand of 2026 are chosen again on that of 2028, and the choice of 2026 reaches 97 per cent of what the choice of 2028 reaches.

\textbf{Land already set aside.} The detailed planning projects shown on the city geoportal \cite{geoportal2026}, compiled from its public map service by members of the project team on 15 October 2026, contain 316 school buildings: 78 stand at existing schools and 238 on sites without one. Fourteen of the 17 located new schools of the plan lie within 500\,m of such a site, and for two of them, on Tokpanov street and at the crossing of Orynbor and Karatobe streets, the city has begun the compulsory acquisition of the land \cite{landacquisition2026}. Built together, the 238 sites would bring a school within 1\,km of 62 per cent of the children who now lack one. The 17 among them that reach the most, solved with Equation~\eqref{eq:mclp} and the mean capacity of the planned schools, would reach 28,873 at 1\,km and 11,726 at 1.5\,km, 2.4 and 3.0 times as many as the plan. The deficit singles out the sites of the detailed plans no better than chance (ROC-AUC 0.51) and density less than chance (0.39): they lie in districts still being built rather than in the served centre. The land for most of the schools the city needs is therefore already reserved on paper; what the plan lacks is an order of construction by the children each site would reach. The last stage of the pipeline publishes the same screening as an interactive web atlas (Figure~\ref{fig:atlas}), which ranks the 238 sites greedily, adding at each step the site that reaches the most children not yet reached; ignoring capacity, which hardly binds, its first 17 reach 28,920 children within 1\,km, as many as the capacitated optimum, and the full ranking is kept with the code as a table.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{A data-driven framework without official microdata.} The work set out to assess access to social infrastructure from open data, and the main methodological finding is that this is feasible when the ancillary sources are chosen for what they encode rather than for their resolution. Polling-station territories encode the distribution of registered residents, are published as law, and change with the city. Building footprints encode where people can live but not whether they do. Satellite-derived population grids encode the past, and a single age structure for the whole city. Combined in a hierarchy anchored to official totals and official age counts, the sources give a surface that reproduces district populations within 10 per cent, and the choice matters for every downstream result: the age structure alone moved the preschool deficit from one district to the blocks of all of them.

\textbf{Measurement choices that change conclusions.} Four choices changed results by large factors: straight-line against walking distance, by a factor of two to three in statutory compliance; the first step of the floating catchment method, which multiplied demand until it was checked against observed enrolment and the official service territories; the land constraint, which shrank the apparent room for better siting from 3.4 times to 19 per cent within 1\,km; and the supply measure, since official design capacity put Esil at 81 per cent of the mean where floor area had put it at 90. In each case the more careful choice was the one that could be checked against an observation.

\textbf{A deficit of distribution, not of places.} Across the city, preschool places exceed attendance: in 2025 the official statistics count 80,521 places for 63,637 children, 79 children per 100 places \cite{stateducation2026}; the cards of the register count 73,419 places for 66,574 children \cite{indigocards2026}, and 428 of the 728 located preschools published free places on a single evening. The waiting list points the same way: on 15 October 2026 the net need of the general list was 19,518 children, 19,308 of them under two years of age, and only 210 aged two or older, the age from which most preschools enrol; the full list, which counts children waiting for a transfer, was 25,621. Yet only 34 per cent of preschool-age children live within 300\,m of a preschool on foot, and the floating catchment index finds 10 per cent of them in significant low-access clusters. The shortage is one of location: places exist, but not near all the children who need them. City-level provision ratios, which compare totals, hide it. Schools suffer both deficits: 1.7 pupils per design place and 18,486 pupils beyond what two shifts can hold, most of them in Saraishyk, alongside the walking deficit concentrated in Esil and Nura.

\textbf{Implications for planning in Astana.} For both schools and preschools Nura, the district that grows fastest, is the worst served, but most of the deficit lies in groups of blocks within districts, so a single district-level indicator of provision misleads. The official service territories add a planning finding of their own: four children in ten are assigned past a nearer school, and in Nura and Esil more than four in ten live farther than 1\,km from the school they are assigned to. Within districts, deficits cluster in identifiable groups of blocks, which suggests that siting can be targeted at a finer scale than the district. The national programme behaved better than a density rule would have and reached 84 per cent of the best that free land allowed within 1\,km; the larger gains lie in reserving land early, since plots that were free in 2023 had largely been built over by 2026, and in building first those sites already reserved in the detailed plans that reach the most children without a school. The construction plan for 2026--2028 repeats the pattern rather than correcting it: it reaches 15 per cent of the children without a school within 1\,km and sends the most places to crowded Saraishyk, while Esil, with the largest walking deficit, gains little; the same number of schools on sites already reserved in the detailed plans would reach 2.4 times as many, and screening sites by the uncovered children they would reach would cost the city nothing. Births point the same way for preschools: they are rising in Nura, already the district with the lowest preschool access, and falling in Baikonur and Saryarka.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Population below the district level is an estimate; equal shares per polling station are an assumption supported by district totals, not an observation, and 46 territorial stations have no reconstructed territory. The Monte Carlo spread of station sizes is calibrated to the district error rather than to observed counts of voters, and the projection to 2028 assumes that the cohort change of 2024--2026 continues. New housing is housing that appears in OSM, which lags construction, and footprints detected only by machine are never counted as new. Age structure is known only by district, so children are spread within a district in proportion to population, and the official split of children between Almaty and Saraishyk is contradicted by enrolment and replaced by their combined share; results under the official split are given alongside. At the level of school territories the population surface cannot be tested against enrolment, because the share of children matched to a territory differs from school to school: the rank correlation is 0.34 to 0.37 whatever the surface. School capacity and enrolment are official, but design capacity counts places in one shift and does not say how many shifts a building can run; preschool capacity and attendance come from the cards of the register compiled by the project team on a single day, and the waiting list is published only for the city as a whole, not by district. Official service territories could be matched to dwellings for 56 per cent of school-age children, less in Nura and Saraishyk, where OSM carries fewer house numbers. Two of the 23 programme schools are placed from listings because OSM does not map them yet, and the official address of school 118 differs from its listed position; the sites of the detailed plans are building contours whose reservation status the geoportal does not give; of the 25 planned projects one could not be located and six are placed only to a street or a nearby address. Floors imputed for untagged buildings are understated by about two floors in new residential complexes, which shifts population between dwellings of a station but, as tested in Section~\ref{sec:population}, not the results. Land ownership is unknown, so free land is land without buildings, not land available for a school. The vacancy label comes from a single evening.
