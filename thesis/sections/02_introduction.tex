\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

\textbf{Relevance.} Astana is growing fast: its population rose from 1.35 million at the start of 2023 to 1.64 million at the start of 2026, and in 2025 alone the district of Nura grew by 23 per cent \cite{statpop2023, astanapop2026}. Housing is built faster than the schools and preschools that its residents need. The national project Comfortable School names Astana among the five regions that account for most of the country's shortage of school places and attributes the persistence of three-shift schooling to construction that is not planned against the growth of the child population \cite{comfortschool2022}. The city reported a shortage of 27,800 school places in 2024 \cite{zakon2024schools}, and the programme completed 23 schools in Astana in 2024 and 2025 \cite{skc2023, skc2024}. Where those schools should stand, and whether children can reach the schools and preschools that exist, are questions of spatial accessibility.

\textbf{State of the problem.} Methods for measuring accessibility, its inequality and the optimal location of new facilities are mature and are reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:context}. Their application rests on data that cities like Astana do not publish: population below the district level, the capacity of individual facilities, and the catchments to which households are assigned. Studies elsewhere take such data from censuses and administrative registers. In their absence an analyst either uses global gridded population products, whose errors are rarely checked locally, or does not attempt the analysis. No fine-grained quantitative assessment of access to social infrastructure in Astana was found in the literature.

\textbf{Aim.} The aim of the work is to develop and validate a data-driven framework that assesses spatial accessibility to social infrastructure, diagnoses its inequity and derives siting recommendations, and to apply it to schools and preschool organisations in Astana using only openly available data.

\textbf{Objectives.} To achieve the aim, the work: assembles from open sources an inventory of schools and preschools and a population surface below the district level and measures their accuracy; computes walking accessibility and statutory compliance; describes the inequality and spatial clustering of accessibility; models revealed demand for preschool places; tests the siting of the national school construction programme against an optimisation model; and tests the stability of all conclusions under alternative data and modelling choices.

\textbf{Object and subject.} The object of research is the spatial configuration of school and preschool provision and of residential demand for it in Astana. The subject is the set of data-driven methods by which access to that provision is measured, its inequity diagnosed and its siting evaluated.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} Four hypotheses are tested (Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}): that open sources estimate population below the district level more accurately than the standard gridded product (H1); that accessibility deficits concentrate in the districts of recent growth (H2); that this pattern is robust to the choice of data and parameters (H3); and that the programme's sites follow deficits rather than density, while a coverage-optimal choice of the same number of sites would have reached more underserved children (H4).

\textbf{Methods.} The work combines record linkage and address geocoding, dasymetric population modelling anchored to official totals and to the territories of polling stations, machine-learning classification of residential buildings, network analysis of walking distances, a floating catchment accessibility index, Gini and Theil inequality measures with spatial autocorrelation statistics, interpretable gradient boosting, and capacitated maximal covering optimisation with land constraints derived from the national planning standard. Every model is validated against an observation it did not use.

\textbf{Scientific novelty.} The work shows that a population surface for a city without published sub-district statistics can be built from the legal description of polling-station territories, and that it is three times more accurate at district level than the standard gridded product. It introduces a load step for the floating catchment index that is calibrated against observed school enrolment. It evaluates a national school construction programme against an optimum restricted to land that was actually free when the sites were chosen, which separates the effect of siting decisions from the effect of land availability.

\textbf{Practical significance.} The framework is open and reproducible by one command from public data, and can be rerun as the city grows. Its results identify the blocks where children lack a school or a preschool within walking distance, distinguish the districts where schools are the binding problem from those where preschools are, and show that the largest gain available to planners lies in reserving land early rather than in choosing better among the plots that remain.

\textbf{Structure.} Chapter~\ref{ch:context} reviews the methods of accessibility measurement, equity analysis, location optimisation and machine learning in urban analytics, sets out the regulatory framework of Kazakhstan, and states the aim, objectives and hypotheses. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} describes the data and the methods, including the test of H1. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports and discusses the results for H2 to H4. The conclusion summarises the findings and the directions for further work. Appendices document the architecture of the framework, its tests and the features of its models.
