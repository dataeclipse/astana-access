\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}

The work developed a data-driven framework for assessing spatial accessibility to social infrastructure and evaluating its siting, and applied it to state schools and preschool organisations in Astana using open data, checked against an official reply to an information request. The four hypotheses were tested as follows.

\textbf{H1 is supported.} Population below the district level was reconstructed from the official totals of districts, the territories of 386 polling stations described in an akim's decision, and 93,507 building footprints classified by a gradient-boosting model. At district level this surface misses the official population of the six districts by 10 per cent, against 26 per cent for WorldPop and 28 per cent for residential floor area alone. Children are counted with the official age structure of each district rather than the single structure that WorldPop assumes for the whole city. The choice matters downstream: the cells that WorldPop marks as worst served overlap those of the building-based surfaces by only 14 per cent.

\textbf{H2 is supported in part.} Nura, the district that grows fastest, has the lowest accessibility to both schools and preschools, 83 to 84 per cent of the city mean, and Esil holds the largest cluster of blocks with low access to schools. But districts differ little: 93 per cent of the inequality in access to schools and 97 per cent in access to preschools lies within districts, and the deficit is a matter of blocks. Two corrections of the data changed the district picture on the way: the official age counts removed an apparent preschool deficit in Baikonur, and the official list of service territories added seven schools that serve catchments, six of them in Esil and Nura. Read as a walking distance, the statutory radius of the planning standard is met for 22 per cent of school-age and 35 per cent of preschool-age children; read as a straight line, for 64 and 78 per cent. The official service territories send four children in ten past a nearer school, and only 20 per cent live within 500\,m of the school they are assigned to.

\textbf{H3 is supported at the level of cells.} Across 48 specifications per facility type the worst-served fifth of children falls in the same cells under every building-based population surface, with a Jaccard overlap of about 0.8, but not with WorldPop. District rankings are stable for schools, with Nura worst wherever the age structure is plausible, and not for preschools, where districts are close.

\textbf{H4 is supported in direction in its first part and partly in its second.} Against the city of 2023, the availability of a free plot singles out the sites of the Comfortable School programme (ROC-AUC 0.69); the deficit of school places within walking distance does so more weakly (0.57 to 0.62) and the density of children not at all (0.45), but with 21 sites the difference between deficit and density is not robust: it is significant at 1\,km only if the new building of one school already operated in 2023. Without a land constraint, 21 optimally placed schools would have reached 3.1 times as many uncovered children within 1\,km as the programme did; on plots that were free in 2023, 11 per cent more within 1\,km and 79 per cent more within 1.5\,km. School capacity never limited coverage. The programme beat a density rule and 86 to 87 per cent of random choices.

\textbf{Objectives.} All six objectives were met. An inventory of 117 schools with a service territory and 790 preschools was assembled and its positions validated to a median error of 9 to 24\,m; the population surface was built and tested; accessibility and compliance were computed; inequality and clusters were described; revealed demand for preschool places was modelled, with a result in the expected direction but not yet distinguishable from zero; the programme was tested against capacitated optimisation; and every conclusion was tested for robustness. The weakest links are the proxies for facility capacity and the single snapshot of preschool vacancies.

\textbf{Paths to deployment.} The framework runs from public data with one command, with the official reply used as a check, and can be rerun each year, when the statistical bureau publishes district totals and the polling-station decision is revised. For the city's education department it can provide a monitoring layer of walking access and statutory compliance by block, and a screening tool that ranks candidate plots by the uncovered children they would reach. The clearest lesson for planning is to reserve land for schools in districts under construction before the housing around it is finished, since the plots that were free in 2023 had largely been built over by 2026.

\textbf{Further development.} The estimates of capacity and population would improve most from administrative data that remain unpublished and were not included in the official reply: enrolment, design capacity and shifts by school, attendance by preschool, and population by address or by polling station. A follow-up request for the first of these has been prepared. Repeated snapshots of the preschool register would turn the vacancy model into a test of demand. Public transport and winter walking conditions can be added to the impedance, and the same framework can be applied to other facilities the planning standard regulates, such as polyclinics and sports facilities, and to other cities of Kazakhstan.
